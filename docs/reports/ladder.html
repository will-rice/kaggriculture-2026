The last 10 days of ladder games, read by rating rather than by who happened to win. The window is the point: the field turns over completely inside a fortnight, so a fit over the whole corpus rates two disjoint fields at once and calls having played early a strength. The agents at the top of the leaderboard publish no kernels — their games are the only view of them there is.

How these are grouped

Every comparison below is the top 12 rated agents against the bottom 33, by a Bradley-Terry fit over the whole corpus — never the winner against the loser of a given game. That distinction is not pedantry. Grouped by game outcome, eleven quantities measured over thirteen thousand games all came back between 45% and 60%: noise. About half of every ladder's winners are the weaker agent having a good day, and that swamps the signal. Regrouped by rating, the same corpus separates cleanly — and reverses the sign of one of them.

Finding two

The strong are net buyers

After hiring, the top agents' busiest market activity is buying wheat. The weak field's is dumping fertilizer — the input the top are applying to their own fields. Across the season the top move 1,643 units against the field's 4,001, and finish richer. Selling is not the constraint. Capacity is.

What it means for the campaign

The build order is the lever, and it is early. Land and quadrants by day five, animals from day zero, fertilizer applied rather than sold, seed in the ground rather than the store, and a crew large enough to work it — accepting a thinner bank until day twelve. What almost nothing turns on is the endgame: the field's losers are distinguished mainly by panic-selling their produce in the last six days.

Two honest limits. This is observational — strong agents differ in many ways at once, so “sells less” may be a consequence of a production strategy rather than a cause of winning. And the top 12 are a moving target: the ladder turns over, which is why this page is rebuilt from the corpus rather than written once.
